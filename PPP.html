Understanding the why

    In the previous section,while we were able to identify dark patterns and some questionable practices, we also thought it was important to understand what led to these decisions in the first place. Hence, we start this section by trying to understand the "why" behind some of the dark patterns.

    Misleading marketing copy

    From “Framing of Information”, we found that UI copies tended to rephrase terms, when they had to be shown directly to users. Why might marketing teams frame a service more favourably than its terms allow?

    • Perhaps technical, restrictive language is seen as a barrier to attracting users.
    • The company may prioritise a clear, appealing message over communicating every limitation upfront.
    • Campaigns may be optimised for sign-ups and conversions, creating pressure to emphasise benefits over conditions.

    Low visibility of terms

    From "Display of Information", we noticed mentions of the privacy policy, terms and conditions made deliberately small and with low contrast.Why might such important terms be displayed in small text or with low contrast?

    • Perhaps making them less prominent is seen as a way to keep the user journey simple and uninterrupted.
    • Designers may struggle to fit extensive legal information into an already crowded screen.
    • Making terms less prominent may help reduce drop-offs during onboarding or transactions.

    Where good intentions can go wrong

    These decisions may begin with seemingly reasonable goals: attracting users or reducing friction. But when those goals take priority over informed choice, they can undermine trust.

    Understanding the pressures a company faces doesn’t justify every decision. It helps us recognise what drives these practices and where change might begin.

    These are our speculations about how these decisions were taken. They are not representative of Phonepe’s actual workings in any way.

    A "purposeful" shift

    Many of these practices can emerge when financial growth and business metrics take precedence over other responsibilities.

    So, we asked, What does a company need to prioritise to create products that are responsible by design?

    We explore this through three essential needs: People, Planet, and Purpose.

    People

    • User agency and flexibility in how my data gets used, tracked and shared with other platforms.
    • Equal opportunities for all to use services, inclusive of disabilities and special needs.
    • The right to informed consent to services, to exit with respect and to choose without manipulation.

    Planet

    • Clear pathways for recycling of hardware waste, to minimize environmental impact as well as manufacturing costs.
    • Conscious use of algorithms that can reduce energy usage for the same task.
    • Assessing impact of features and services with respect to the impact on company's carbon footprint, to prioritize and filter out extras.

    Purpose

    • Accountability of the platform as well as its partners in case of promises, promotions and faults.
    • Honest promotion of value to users rather than deceptive persuasion
    • Clarity and support when decisions are likely to affect users significantly./li>

    People

    PhonePe makes it easy to sign up and share data, but hard to understand what happens to that data, to get help, or to leave. Our People recommendations put users back in control.

    Recommendation 1

    Help users make informed financial decisions rather than focusing solely on engagement with services.

    Recommendation 2

    Make service exits and account deactivation free from unnecessary friction. Introducing difficulties reduces user autonomy and leads to a loss of trust.

    Recommendation 3

    Exploiting inactivity and human errors should be avoided at all costs. Transparency in consequences and reasoning for data usage is essential for long term trust.

    Planet

    PhonePe runs on servers, stored data and physical devices, and much of it keeps running even when nobody is using it. Our Planet recommendations cut out what isn’t needed.

    Recommendation 1

    Create clarity in recycling routes, with prompts and promotions that explain the process to regular hardware users.

    Recommendation 2

    Penalties for inactive services create forced usage and long term data stagnancy. Reminders to users can help cut down extra running costs for Phonepe.

    Recommendation 3

    Features like AI chatbots take up large amounts of energy, with low user engagement due to dead ends. Diverse query paths and anticipatory support can help make better use of these resources.

    Purpose

    PhonePe’s terms are mostly written to protect the platform: they limit its responsibility, keep important rules out of sight, and send users elsewhere when things go wrong. Our Purpose recommendations make PhonePe honest.

    Recommendation 1

    Define the line between third party marketing and self promotion. Marketing of collaborators and Phonepe's services should be easy to differentiate.

    Recommendation 2

    Updates and changes to terms that can affect users directly, deserve to be shown upfront. Timely information, in-app reminders and easy navigation through relevant terms should be essential aspects of the service.

    Recommendation 3

    Accountability and clear distinction of responsibilities is integral to an aggregator like Phonepe. Users should not feel lost when reaching out for help.

    Pitch it!

    When we made recommendations based on the needs identified from People, Planet and Purpose, we couldn't help but wonder if they would still stand against the realities of a company. To reevaluate and strenghten our recommendations, we decided to bring the three lenses Ethical, Professional, and Design to our help. These then translated into 3 distinct departments in a company itself, as shown below.

    Now we explore how our recommendations could get questioned by each of them. Scroll to see how the conversation goes!

    Recommendation 1 · People

    Make leaving as easy as joining: exits and account deactivation should be free from unnecessary friction.

    P
    Presenter

    Our first recommendation: a clear “Deactivate account” option, with no hidden menus and no support call required to leave.

    L
    Legal

    Are we required to offer this, or is it a nice-to-have? And what about records we must keep for financial regulations?

    P
    Presenter

    Both can be true. Data protection principles point towards making withdrawal of consent as easy as giving it. Deactivation doesn’t have to mean deleting everything. We just need to say plainly what is retained, and why.

    B
    Business

    Every exit is a lost user. Why would we make leaving easier?

    P
    Presenter

    Forced retention isn’t loyalty. Users who know they can leave freely are more likely to join, and more likely to return.

    D
    Design

    We can offer a “pause instead” option without guilt-tripping. One screen: what happens to your data, balance and linked accounts, then a clear confirm button. Alternatives sit beside the exit, never in front of it.

    L
    Legal

    I can support that, as long as the retention notice is accurate and reviewed.

    Takeaways

    • Legal’s concern is about what we keep and say, not about whether exits should exist.
    • Business objections soften when retention is reframed as trust rather than lock-in.
    • Design makes the compromise real: alternatives are offered beside the exit, never in its way.

    Recommendation 2 · Planet

    Anticipate user problems and offer diverse query paths, so energy-hungry AI support isn’t spent on dead ends.

    P
    Presenter

    Chatbots use a lot of energy, yet users often hit a dead end and start over. We recommend surfacing likely issues first and giving the chatbot more ways to resolve a query.

    B
    Business

    Is this a sustainability project or a support-cost project? If engagement is low, why not simply remove the chatbot?

    P
    Presenter

    It’s both, and removal just moves the problem. A dead end means the same issue is raised again, then escalated to a human. That is wasted compute and wasted support time.

    D
    Design

    Put the common problems up front, such as failed payments and refund status, so many people never need to type. The chatbot becomes the fallback, with a visible route to a human.

    L
    Legal

    If the bot explains refunds or responsibility, the wording matters. Escalation paths and who answers for what need to be documented.

    B
    Business

    Can we measure whether it works?

    P
    Presenter

    Yes: queries per resolved issue, repeat-contact rate and handoff rate. Fewer retries means lower energy use and lower cost.

    Takeaways

    • Sustainability lands best when it is tied to a metric the business already tracks.
    • Preventing the problem beats optimising the chatbot that answers it.
    • A visible human handoff is good design and good risk management.

    Recommendation 3 · Purpose

    Show changes to terms that affect users upfront: in the app, in good time, and in plain language.

    P
    Presenter

    When terms change in ways that affect money, data or responsibility, users should be told in the app, not left to find out later.

    L
    Legal

    We already publish updated terms on our website. Isn’t that enough?

    P
    Presenter

    Published isn’t the same as noticed. In our audit, links to terms sat in tiny, low-contrast text where almost nobody would read them.

    B
    Business

    A pop-up for every update hurts conversion and annoys people.

    D
    Design

    So we tier it. Interruptions are reserved for changes that touch money, data or liability. Everything else gets a dismissible notice and a short “what changed” summary.

    L
    Legal

    I can work with a tiered approach if the full terms stay linked and dated.

    P
    Presenter

    Then we agree: clear when it matters, quiet when it doesn’t.

    Takeaways

    • “We published it” is a legal position. “Users saw it” is a trust position.
    • Tiering notices answers Business’s fear of friction without hiding what matters.
    • Each lens changed the proposal, and the final version is stronger for it.
