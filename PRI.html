Digitally responsible design principles

Drawing on everything we learned from our recommendations and the interests we found we share, these are the principles we drafted to guide digitally responsible design. Each one is paired with the ethos statements we each commit to.

01

The right to Informed Consent

Defining the gap between informed and formal consent.

We believe

Agreeing to a service is meaningful only when people can anticipate the important consequences of that agreement, including costs and obligations that arise later.

So we will

Identify and present relevant future consequences when users are taking significant financial decisions

Instead of

Treating a user's initial acceptance of lengthy terms as sufficient consent for consequences they may encounter much later.

Ethos statements to uphold the principle

Each of us has written what upholding this principle means to us personally.

L
Tanaya

I will advocate for making consequential conditions visible and understandable before users commit.

Even when simplifying the journey would improve conversion rates

Which has cost me the simplicity of a designed flow and smooth opting in for services for my users

B
Dhun

I will ask what a person is really agreeing to, and what it could mean for them later, before I decide how the agreement looks.

Even when I’m not the one making the call and the push for a shorter flow comes quietly, as “that’s how it’s always done.”

Which has cost me 1) being seen as someone who complicates simple decisions, and 2) extra rounds of review just to get consequences onto the screen.

02

The freedom to disengage

Reducing dependence on user engagement as the sole success metric

We believe

a product earns continued use through the value it provides; a user's attention, inactivity or decision to leave should not become leverage against them.

So we will

communicate genuine consequences upfront, support the transfer or retrieval of user information where appropriate, and ensure that retention efforts do not pressure a user's decision to leave.

Instead of

using friction, financial penalties or increasingly persuasive incentives to preserve engagement regardless of the user's intentions.

Ethos statements to uphold the principle

Each of us has written what upholding this principle means to us personally.

L
Tanaya

I will challenge design decisions that make it harder for users to leave or reduce their participation

Even when adding friction could improve retention metrics and on paper engagement

Which has cost me perceived loss of business due to design, and reliance on lagging indicators for success

B
Dhun

I will treat a person’s decision to leave as something worth designing well, not a failure to recover from

Even when the metrics my team is measured on depend on people staying, and I may not have the standing to question them.

D
Siddhi

Even when growth numbers would look better if people had to dig around to quit.

Which has cost me a nicer-looking retention chart.

03

Anticipatory Support systems

Defining accountability and creating anticipatory help routes

We believe

When a product brings together multiple service providers, users deserve clarity on who is responsible for each part of their experience, especially when something goes wrong.

So we will

Clearly identify third-party providers, distinguish their responsibilities from the platform's own, and guide users to the appropriate support and resolution process when an issue arises.

Instead of

Leaving users to identify which provider is responsible, navigate disconnected support systems, or determine who can resolve their issue on their own.

Ethos statements to uphold the principle

Each of us has written what upholding this principle means to us personally.

L
Tanaya

I will advocate for clear paths to resolution and avoiding dead ends

Even when a problem falls outside the product team's or company's direct responsibility

Which has cost me additional time, coordination with other teams, or the convenience of drawing a clear boundary around my role.

B
Dhun

I will work out who is responsible for each part of an experience before deciding how seamless it should feel

Even when the partnerships behind it are shaped by agreements I can’t see and the brief asks for one unified brand.

D
Siddhi

I will show people exactly who to contact if something goes wrong, before it goes wrong, not after.

Even when that makes the app feel less like one smooth, all-in-one experience.

Which has cost me that polished feeling of everything being handled by us alone.

04

Transparency in data usage

Distinguishing between legal obligations and business purposes for data sharing

We believe

People should be able to understand why their data is retained and how it is used, without having to distinguish legal obligations from business choices buried in broad privacy terms themselves.

So we will

Clearly distinguish data practices required by law from those used for personalisation, marketing and service improvement, explaining their purposes, applicable retention periods and available user choices.

Instead of

Grouping different data practices under vague, blanket explanations that leave users uncertain about what the platform must do, what it chooses to do, and what control they have.

Ethos statements to uphold the principle

Each of us has written what upholding this principle means to us personally.

L
Tanaya

I will question data practices whose purposes or necessity remain unclear

Even when they are legally permissible or commercially valuable

Which has cost me the convenience of accepting existing practices and revenue opportunities from data sharing

B
Dhun

I will ask whether a data practice is required of us or chosen by us before I help explain it

Even when the reasoning sits with legal or business teams and the honest answer may be hard to get.

Which has cost me 1) time spent chasing answers before I can design anything, and 2) being seen as stepping into decisions that aren’t “design’s” to make.

D
Siddhi

I will tag every data request with a plain label like “required by law” or “optional, helps us personalise” which will help people take better decisions.

Even when that makes it obvious how much data collection isn’t actually required.

Which has cost me some of the data my team is used to getting by default.
